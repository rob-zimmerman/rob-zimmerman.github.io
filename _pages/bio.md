I grew up on a farm just outside a small town in Ontario. It wasn’t much of a town at all, really, but the old-timers would tell that it had once been a prosperous place. The story went that during the Gold Rush, some prospector passing through had noticed a rich vein of gold in the hills. Folks came by from all over to try their luck, and for some time the town was the talk of the province. All I knew was that by the time I came around, there wasn’t much gold left. There wasn’t much of anything left, really.

Pa ran the farm with help from Momma and us children. We had a few cows, a healthy flock of chickens and ducks, a few rabbits, and a field of barley that never really grew as well as Pa had hoped. Most mornings began at six o’clock sharp, when our rooster Roger would work his way to the top of the barn and start crowing, just like in the movies. I don’t think Roger knew that was his name, but he was a dependable fellow all the same.

I spent most of my days milking the cows and collecting eggs from the chickens. Processing the rabbits was never fun, but we did what we had to do. When I got bored, my brothers and I would play jacks or go exploring in the woods beyond the farm. One evening, we snuck out and took Pa’s pickup for a joyride around town. We felt Pa’s belt after that one, but he forgave us eventually. We never heard the end of it from Momma though.

Eventually I attended the little schoolhouse down the street, where Miss DuBois taught us reading, writing, arithmetic, and proper manners. Boy, did I hate it! Miss DuBois didn’t know too much about mathematics or science, or anything all that fancy, but she sure was particular about how a gentleman ought to hold a knife and fork. I wonder if she’s still around. She was getting on in years even then.

Times were hard back then. I remember when the Spanish flu came through town and took poor Uncle Clem. He’d driven into town to sell some of his wooden toys and kitchen utensils and came home feeling mighty poorly. At first we hoped that it was just a touch of the ague, but within a week he was gone. Clem had never even been to Spain!

Things got worse when the Dust Bowl came around. The barley withered, the cows’ milk stopped flowing, and before long we hadn’t enough grain to feed the fowl. Poor Roger passed away, and we had to take turns waking each other up. Pa tried planting something else — I forget what — but that didn’t take neither. Momma earned a few dollars a month mending torn up clothes for the neighbours, and for some time we got by on spuds and whatever other hardy vegetables we could manage to grow in the garden. Pa said things would turn around soon enough. He was usually right about those things, although not always as soon as he’d hoped.

When the war broke out, some army men came by the farm looking for young fellows to fight the Krauts. My brothers were drafted, but they passed me over on account of my scoliosis. Pa said it was the first time that crooked back of mine had ever done me any good. Momma cried when the boys left, but she was proud of them all the same. Edwin eventually came back with a leg missing but Frederick was lucky enough to go unscathed.

I remember one evening some years later, Pa gathered us around the dinner table. Giddy with excitement, he announced that he’d bought us a television. Momma was right furious at the expense, but Pa was insistent that a family ought to know what was happening in the world. We set it up in the sitting room, and from then on we could watch the evening news whenever the picture came in clearly enough.

One evening, the newsman announced that the Americans had put a man on the moon. I thought that sure was a mighty peculiar thing to do. Pa didn’t believe a word of it, and neither did I. After supper, I went outside and looked up at the moon for a good long while. I certainly couldn’t see anybody.

As the years passed, I began to think about the future, and what I might do with my life. I sure loved the farm, but I’d always been curious about the world beyond our little town. Pa wanted me to take over the place one day, but I wasn’t sure I had the temperament for it. I was more interested in the machines that were starting to turn up everywhere. Some of them could do in ten minutes what would take us the better part of an afternoon.

Soon after, the telephone company came around and offered to hook the farmhouse up to something called the Internet. Pa wanted nothing to do with it. He said that we already had a telephone, and he didn’t see why it needed to be hooked up to anything else. Then the Berlin Wall came down, and the neighbours called us over to see it on their computer. There were hundreds of folks cheering and dancing atop the thing, chipping away at it with hammers and chisels. Pa became a believer right then and there! I couldn’t quite see what all the excitement was about. We had an old stone wall behind the barn that me and Pa had been meaning to tear down for years, and nobody ever made such a fuss about that.

Then, in ’08, the financial crisis struck. We’d heard on the news that those fancy businessmen down in New York had gotten themselves into a right awful mess, betting on things they didn’t understand. They’d been lending money to poor folks who couldn’t pay it back, and then selling those debts to other businessmen, who’d sell them to other businessmen, and so on and so forth, until nobody seemed to know who owed what to whom! Then the money stopped coming in, and the whole thing came tumbling down. What tickled me the most was that those businessmen had apparently hired some of the finest mathematics folks in the world to help them with their figuring, and yet none of them seemed to have seen it coming. Even Pa knew better than to count on a good harvest before the barley had come out of the ground!

I got to thinking that there must be something to that mathematics business after all. Up until then, the most mathematics I’d ever had to do was count how many dozen eggs we’d sold and multiply that by twelve cents. But here were men making fortunes doing sums I couldn’t even begin to understand. They’d lost most of their fortunes, sure, but I supposed that was a separate matter.

So I finally made up my mind to leave the farm and go off to the city to study mathematics. I wasn’t sure quite what I’d do with it, but I hoped that with a bit of hard work and a little luck, I might make something of myself. Momma was proud, though she cried when I told her. Pa wasn’t so sure. He reckoned that a man was better off making a living with his hands than sitting behind a desk all day scratching numbers onto pieces of paper.

Maybe he was right. But I’d taken a fancy to the idea of making a living counting chickens before they’d hatched.

Pa always warned me against that.
